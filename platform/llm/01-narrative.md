# The story
Ten chapters. Each paragraph is followed by the records it rests on.

## 0. Before the beginning — Radomyśl Wielki  
*1850—1886*

For a year this family could go back no further than a name on a passport. The paternal line stopped at Berisz Rapaport, and even Edward Gelles, who spent twenty-five years on the Rapaport rabbinical genealogies, could not carry it past him. In May 2026 Basia found the passport file itself, and inside it were his parents: Moses Saul Rapaport and Menukha.

> Sources: Berisz Rapaport passport application, Stanisławów 1924

Berisz was born on 30 July 1886 in Radomyśl Wielki, and the family's registered commune was Dąbrowa Tarnowska — a town nobody could account for until July 2026, when Basia read the Tarnów notarial records and found Szabse Rapaport serving as rabbi of Dąbrowa until 20 April 1884. That is why the passport says Dąbrowa. The same evening, from a completely different direction, Dana was sent a record naming Schabse with Figa/Yete.

> Sources: Tarnów notarial records (Basia, July 2026); MyHeritage record, 7 July 2026

Three marriage deeds from Tarnów — 1919, 1920 and 1923, for three of Berisz's sisters — each independently name the same couple as their parents. Three separate clerks, in three different years, writing the same two names. That is as close to certainty as this kind of research reaches.

> Sources: Tarnów marriage registers 1919 (entry 40), 1920 (entry 98), 1923 (entry 101)

Behind all of it now stands a spine of paper. Fifty-eight civil-register photographs came from Basia's archive on 6 August 2026 — twenty-seven births, eleven marriages, nineteen deaths — running from a birth in 1818 to a death in 1934. They are the ordinary machinery of a town: a clerk writing down that a child was born, that two people married, that someone died. Read together they carry six generations of this family from Abraham Abe, born 1784, down to the parents of the man this archive is written for. The naming was tested before any of it was believed: twenty-seven of the twenty-eight birth records carry the same year this archive already held for that person. One does not — a birth filed 1849 where we hold 1850 — and it is published as the disagreement it is.

> Sources: 58 civil-register photographs, Basia’s family archive, received 6 August 2026

## 1. Nadwórna — David  
*1911*

David Mendel Rapaport — Memek — was born in Nadwórna on 25 December 1911, son of Berisz Rapaport and Rebeka Griffel. Nadwórna sat in the Carpathian foothills of eastern Galicia, then Austria-Hungary, then Poland, today Nadvirna in Ukraine.

> Sources: Nadwórna civil birth register

His Brussels Displaced Persons card of 1946 gives his birthplace as "Cieszyn (Tesin)" — a town four hundred kilometres away, where no one in this family ever lived. It is a transcription error by a French clerk, and it is worth pausing on: here the document is wrong and the family's memory is right. That is why nothing in this archive ranks paper above testimony as a rule.

> Sources: DIPIS card, Brussels 1946; Nadwórna civil birth register

## 2. Bolechów — Lusia  
*1913—1933*

Leah Weitzner — Lusia — was born in Bolechów on 8 April, daughter of Elias (Eliyahu) Weitzner and Matel (Matylda) née Weinreb. Every record agrees on the day. They do not agree on the year: the Bolechów civil register says 1913, while her own survivor card, the ŻIH index and the Haifa burial register all say 1916. Three against two, but the two include the civil register. It remains unresolved.

> Sources: Bolechów birth register 1913; CKŻP card nr 151738; Haifa Chevra Kadisha register

In 1933 her parents left for Mandatory Palestine. Lusia stayed. It is a small sentence with the whole of what follows inside it: she would not see them again for fifteen years, and the war would fall on the side of the family that remained.

> Sources: Memoir ch.1 — family testimony, no document held

## 3. The marriage, and Shimon  
*1935—1938*

They married on 10 February 1935 in Bolechów, her own town. She was twenty-one and he was twenty-three.

> Sources: Civil marriage record, Bolechów, 10 February 1935

Her memoir puts the wedding in Muszyna, in February 1938 — three years later and in another town. Written forty years afterwards, the memory placed the marriage at the opening of the Muszyna years rather than three years before them. The month is right in both. And the record resolves something the memoir left broken: under her date, Shimon arrives eight months before his parents marry; under the register, he is born two and a half years into the marriage.

> Sources: Memoir p.16; Civil marriage record 1935

Shimon was born on 22 June 1937 in Lwów. The family had long said "Mosina" — a name that turned out to be Muszyna — but his own survivor card, filled in after the war, says Lwów.

> Sources: CKŻP survivor card nr 151337

## 4. The war  
*1939—1944*

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. What the family did over the next five years is known almost entirely from Lusia's testimony, because almost no one who could have written it down survived to do so.

> Sources: Memoir chs.2-6

She tells of Przemyśl under Soviet administration; of the Nadwórna ghetto and David's escape from it hidden in a wooden wagon; of false identities in Lwów; of the Hormak family, and of neighbours named Pietrozycki and Ivanov who helped them; and of Shimon, three years old, kept in a crate for two and a half years so that he would not be seen or heard. No document we hold corroborates any of it. That is not a weakness in the account — it is what happens when the people who would have kept the records are the people who were killed.

> Sources: Memoir chs.3-6 — no corroborating record held

Two things from those years are on paper. David was stripped of Polish citizenship in 1942. And his father Berisz was murdered at Auschwitz — not on an approximate date but on a known one. He was given prisoner number 27310, arrived at the camp on 27 March 1942 and was dead on 29 March. Two days. Two independent records say so and agree exactly: the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum's victim page, and the camp's own Sterbebücher, the death books kept for registered prisoners. The register adds one word that matters — his residence, Przemyśl. Lusia's memoir, written from memory decades later, says David's parents were in Przemyśl when the war came and that the Germans killed one of them there. A German clerk writing at the time put him in the same town. Neither could have copied the other. The camp recorded his occupation as robotnik, labourer; he was an industrialist in Stanisławów, and that word is the camp's, not his. And we now know where he was taken from. His transport, number 689, was fifty-five men from the PRISON IN TARNÓW, registered together on 27 March 1942 with numbers 27305 to 27359 and sent by the Security Police of the Kraków district; Berisz was the sixth number in that block. Eighteen of the fifty-five were recorded as Jewish and seventeen of them died; at least forty-one of the fifty-five did. So he was not seized in Przemyśl and sent east — he was held in Tarnów, a short way from Radomyśl Wielki where he was born, in the town whose address the family still carried in 1924: Folwarczna 9.

> Sources: Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum victim page (prisoner 27310); Auschwitz Sterbebücher 1941–1943, source record 49478; Lusia’s memoir, ch. 24; DIPIS card, Brussels 1946; Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum, transport 689 — read at source 6 August 2026

And they were remembered. For a long time this page said that no Page of Testimony had ever been filed at Yad Vashem for Berisz or for Rebeka, and that this archive was their only written memorial anywhere. That was wrong — and not because the evidence was missing. Both pages were already here, attached to their own records in this archive's document list: No. 53072 for Bernard, No. 53074 for Regina, each signed on 26 April 1953. The story simply had not been read against the file it was built on. Basia's copies, which arrived on 6 August 2026, are what finally prompted the check. Berisz's page names his father Moshe and his mother Menucha, gives his trade as merchant and sets his death in 1940 — eleven years after the fact, and two years early. Rebeka's gives her birth as 1892 in Nadwórna and her death as 1943; this archive holds 1888 and 1942. Those dates disagree and are left disagreeing. What does not disagree is that in the spring of 1953 somebody who had known them sat down and wrote their names on a form so they would not be lost. They were never unremembered. We had simply not looked at what we were holding. And now we know whose hand. The declaration at the foot of both pages is a filer living in Haifa, signing on 26 April 1953, and where the form asks what he was to the person it says בנו on his father’s page and בנה on his mother’s — his son, her son. Berish and Rebeka had one son. David wrote them. Seven years after he came ashore he sat down and filled in two forms for his parents, and put his father’s death two years wrong, because he was writing from memory about people he had last seen before the war.

> Sources: Yad Vashem Page of Testimony 53072 (Bernard Rapaport), signed 26 April 1953 — held in this archive as doc_bernard_dov_yv_pot; Yad Vashem Page of Testimony 53074 (Regina Rapaport), signed 26 April 1953 — held in this archive as doc_regina_rivka_yv_pot; The submitter block of both Pages of Testimony, read 11 August 2026

## 5. Katowice — and a separation nobody recorded  
*1945—1946*

The war ended and they came west with the repatriation, to Katowice. On 1 July 1946 Lusia and Shimon each filed a survivor-registration card at the Katowice committee — cards 151738 and 151337, mother and son, one after the other.

> Sources: CKŻP cards nr 151738 and nr 151337, Katowice, 1 July 1946

But David had been registered as a Displaced Person in Brussels since 9 April 1946 — three months earlier. On the day he was recorded in Belgium, his wife and son were still in Poland. He had gone ahead, and they followed later that year. The memoir says only that "the family reaches Brussels and stays in a hotel": one movement, everyone together. The two cards, held in this archive since May and never read against each other until August 2026, say otherwise. It is the first of the two separations in this story.

> Sources: DIPIS card, 9 April 1946; CKŻP cards, 1 July 1946; Memoir p.62

## 6. Brussels — and Dov  
*1946*

And she was there first. For as long as this archive has existed, the earliest Brussels record in it was David’s Displaced Persons card of 9 April 1946. On 19 August 2026 Basia sent Lusia’s: she registered in the same city on 26 DECEMBER 1945, three and a half months earlier, signing it "Rapaport Lea" while the clerk filed her under her maiden name. Two days later a Jewish aid society asked the Belgians to let her stay six months; on 7 February 1946 the Ministry of Justice agreed, on condition the permission was marked "with a view to emigration" — she could be in Belgium only while waiting to leave it. She was living at 250 rue des Coteaux in Schaerbeek. Dov was born under that arrangement. Her card carries two things nobody can yet explain: it says she came from a camp in Prague, which appears nowhere else and not in her own book; and it gives her birthplace as Haifa, Palestine. She was born in Bolechów. David’s card of the same series names Cieszyn, also wrong. Two cards, two wrong birthplaces — and on hers, under destination, the word Palestine.

> Sources: doc_lusia_1945_dp_card; doc_lusia_1945_residence_request; doc_lusia_1945_stay_permit_1946; doc_david_dp_card

David's Displaced Persons card describes a forestry engineer, Polish nationality stripped in 1942, speaking Polish, Italian, German, Czech and Russian. It is the only document that reaches across from the war into what came after.

> Sources: DIPIS card, Brussels, 9 April 1946

Dov Bernard Rapaport was born in Brussels on 28 August 1946. No Belgian birth registration is held in this archive — it is the easiest missing document of all, and only he can request it, as the person the record belongs to.

> Sources: Family (Doron, 2026); Memoir p.62

One thing about Belgium had been sitting in plain sight. David's aunt Jente — his father's sister — spent the war in Liège, at Rue Lairesse 88, registered as a Polish national. Her daughter Szewa married there in 1941, a marriage this archive already held without seeing what stood behind it. So when David and Lusia reached Belgium in 1946, it was not a country the family had never been to: part of it had lived there straight through the occupation, a hundred kilometres from Brussels. Whether David went where he had people, no document in our hands says. It is worth asking and it is not worth inventing.

> Sources: Wartime name record 4494611 (Jenta Rapaport, Rue Lairesse 88, Liège); Liège marriage of Szewa Horowitz and Markus Türkel, 1941

## 7. Sète, the Theodor Herzl, Cyprus  
*1947—1948*

On 2 April 1947 David and Shimon sailed from Sète in southern France aboard the Theodor Herzl — the ex-British cable-layer HMS Guardian, built at Newcastle in 1907, carrying 2,641 ma'apilim gathered from camps across France and Belgium. Shimon was nine. Lusia stayed behind in Brussels with the seven-month-old Dov.

> Sources: Memoir p.63; Palyam / ship histories — the vessel is fully documented

On 13 April the Royal Navy boarded her off the coast. The passengers fought; three were killed and twenty-seven wounded. Everyone aboard was deported to Cyprus, and David and Shimon were interned at Karaolos, north of Famagusta, for about eight months. The camps there held 53,510 Jews behind wire between 1946 and 1949, and some two thousand children were born inside them. Shimon was released from Atlit in 1948.

> Sources: Memoir pp.63-66; Cyprus internment camp figures, 1946-1949

## 8. Haifa  
*1948—1996*

Lusia sailed from Brussels with Dov on the Kadima. She had waited more than a year alone with the baby, working, while David and Shimon were behind wire. The Haifa burial register records her year of immigration as 1948 and David's as 1946 — the two-year gap the memoir describes, written into their own graves. Dov's first journey was with his mother, the two of them.

> Sources: Memoir p.66; Haifa Chevra Kadisha register

In Haifa she saw her parents for the first time since 1933. The family settled on Abbas Street, and later bought an apartment on Moriah Street with German reparations — money David argued with himself about taking. He worked as a customs broker, the trade his son Dov followed, and then his grandson Doron.

> Sources: Memoir pp.67-76

In 1986 a granddaughter, Hadas, asked her teacher Esther Weiss for help with a school assignment, and Esther sat down and interviewed Lusia. Ninety pages in Hebrew. Without them, almost nothing in this archive between 1939 and 1948 would have a voice at all.

> Sources: The memoir, compiled 1986

David died on 29 August 1990 and Lusia on 28 December 1996. They are buried beside one another at Sde Yehoshua, graves 102ד and 102ג. Her burial record names her father Eliyahu and her mother Matilda — the same two people her memoir opens with, sixty years earlier and in another language. The last page of the record matches the first page of the testimony.

> Sources: Haifa Chevra Kadisha register; Memoir ch.1

## 9. Going back  
*2015 – 2019*

In April 2015 Dov flew to Poland with his son Doron. They spent a week in Warsaw — the Old Town, the Barbican, the Royal Castle, a city flattened and put back stone by stone — and on 19 April they drove to Przemyśl. That is not a sightseeing stop. Przemyśl is the town where Berish and Rebeka were living when the war came; it is the residence the Auschwitz death books give for Berish, and it is where Lusia’s memoir says the Germans killed one of David’s parents on a fabricated charge. Most of the forty photographs from that day are taken through a car windscreen, driving its streets slowly, in the rain.

> Sources: Dov and Doron, Warsaw and Przemyśl albums, April 2015; Auschwitz Sterbebücher — residence Przemyśl; Lusia’s memoir, ch. 24

In June 2018 he went to Brussels, where he was born on 28 August 1946 — four months after his father filled in a Displaced Persons card in that city and wrote "Palestine or U.S.A." in the box for where he wanted to go. In January 2019 he went to Katowice, in the snow, and photographed it from above and on foot: the town of the separation nobody recorded, where the family was together before David and Shimon went one way and Lusia and the baby another. One hundred and twenty photographs from the four journeys are in this archive.

> Sources: Dov and Doron, Brussels and Katowice albums, 2018 and 2019; DIPIS card, Brussels, 9 April 1946

It is worth saying what that means for an archive like this one. Every other chapter here is assembled from what other people wrote down — clerks, registrars, a camp administration, a memoir. This is the only chapter in which somebody in the story goes back and looks for himself, and the pictures are his. Nothing in them was recovered. They were made.

> Sources: doc_dov_journeys_back
