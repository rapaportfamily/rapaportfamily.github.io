# THE FAMILY IN THE NEWSPAPERS, 1866-1946

David Rapaport's mother was Rebeka Griffel. Her father was Lajzor (Leizor) Griffel of Nadwórna, and it is his shop, his refinery, his sawmill, his sons and his grandchildren that these notices follow for eighty years — from a 50-kreuzer war donation in 1866 to the nationalisation of a sawmill in 1946. Berish Rapaport, David's father, appears in 1912 holding the power of procuration in a Griffel firm, and in 1913 as half of a new drilling company called "Griffel jun. et Rappaport".

142 notices from 128 pages of Polish and German press, located and transcribed by Basia and translated in full for this archive. Each one below gives the year, the paper it appeared in, and what it says. The Polish or German original of every one, and the Hebrew, are on the site at https://rapaportfamily.github.io/#/press

This is where the family stops being a list of dates and becomes people with a reputation in a town. Quote it freely; every line traces to a named newspaper and issue.

**6 June 1907 — Wielkopolanin, official organ of the Union of St Joseph in Pittsburgh, Pa., No. 23, Thursday 6 June 1907**  
NADWÓRNA. On the night of Thursday to Friday, 9 May, a fire broke out in our town [text corrupted — B.S.] in the Pniów suburb. The whole block of houses between Młyńska and Szkolna streets burned down, belonging to the poorest of the Jewish population. The fire began at half past ten at night in a bakery standing among those little houses and spread in an instant through all of them, consuming 53 houses and 8 [text corrupted — B.S.]. There was wind; otherwise the whole town would have gone up in smoke. The little houses that burned were extremely poor and built close together. The synagogues, the so-called brick schools, resisted the blaze to the extent that the walls remained whole, but the roofs and interiors burned out. The damage was uninsured except for a few houses. Destitution reigns among the inhabitants struck by the fire: about seven hundred people are left without a roof. On the first news of the fire the Viceroy sent 5,000 crowns in aid for the victims, Mr Zipser of Dobromil 200 crowns, and the committee headed by the district governor Mr Marek has so far collected 1,700 crowns. The collections are being managed by Mr Duduszyński, councillor of the provincial court, Mr Loegler, station master, and LEIZOR GRIFFEL.
*fire · communal leadership · the town itself*

**15 June 1913 — Ropa: organ of the association of drilling technicians, No. 11, vol. V, 15 June 1913, Borysław, year III**  
PASIECZNA. Because drilling has begun at the mine of the East Galician Petroleum Company at Pasieczna, drilling activity here has revived somewhat, though it has not reached a proper scale for want of workers. They are deterred by the twelve-hour shift and the relatively low pay. Drillers receive 4-6 crowns a shift here, assistants 1-1.50 crowns. At Bitków — where a shortage of workers is also felt — wage conditions are 50 per cent better. At the "A. Compes & Co" mines preparations are being made to deepen many shafts. The present state of the work is as follows. East Galician Petroleum Company, shaft No. 3: drilling at 9 m in hard rock, 18-inch casing; this shaft is being drilled by the Canadian system and not by flushing as previously intended. THE "L. GRIFFEL" MINE, shaft No. 7: drilling at 288 m in shale with sandstone, 9-inch casing, traces of oil. Shaft No. 8: now 142 m deep, 9-inch casing, water shut off at 132 m with 9-inch casing, production 1,500-2,000 kg a day. The "R. Rudolf" mine, shaft No. 2 (No. 15): drilling at 30 m in hard rock, 12-inch casing with plates. The "H. Wundermann and Co" mine, shaft No. 1: now 78 m deep, 7-inch casing, sandstone with shale, water shut off at 52 m with 7-inch casing, producing about 350-400 kg a day. Besides this, a new company has been formed here for the exploitation of oil under the name "GRIFFEL JUN. ET RAPPAPORT". This company intends to begin drilling with three rigs, two machine-driven and one by hand.

**7 April 1922 — The truth about the forest contracts: speech of deputy Wincenty Witos delivered in the Legislative Sejm on 7 April 1922 (Cieszyn, Polish People's Party, 1922)**  
From a speech in the Polish parliament defending the timber companies against deputy Stapiński. Witos states that under Austrian rule the state forests of Little Poland were exploited exclusively by Jews: of 40 contracts covering all the state forests, 32 were held by Jewish entrepreneurs, 4 by Jewish companies, and on 4 more there were nominal front men, though in fact they belonged to Jews. **11 November 1923 — Kurier Stanisławowski, year 36, no. 174 (18 November 1923)**  
FIRE AT GRIFFEL'S REFINERY. Last Sunday about 3.15 in the afternoon the Municipal Fire Brigade was called to a fire at GRIFFEL'S REFINERY AT MYKIETYŃCE and left at once. On the spot it was established that the fire had broken out, for reasons so far unascertained, in the receiving room, spreading to all the receivers in which kerosene and benzine were burning, as well as the crude materials in the distilling boiler. The Voluntary Railway Fire Brigade also came, together with the military emergency detachment of the 11th field artillery regiment under Captain Suski, the State Police from Knihinin Kolonia and Stanisławów, and representatives of the authorities headed by Mr Dąbrowski. The immediate arrival of Mr Ehrlich, director of the "Port" insurance company, who did his duty on the spot, should also be noted. Our editors sent their own reporter, who was able to see with what real self-sacrifice the men worked to contain the fire. Railway locomotives drew the crude-oil tank cars out of the works, so aiding the rescue operation, which — with notable help from the refinery workers — proceeded briskly under the experienced and expert direction of chief Voelpel, to whom the experts on the spot expressed their appreciation. Explosions of enormous size frightened nobody off, and at the risk of their lives the fire was completely extinguished after two hours. THE DAMAGE, RUNNING TO THREE MILLIARD, IS INSURED. Griffel's refinery paid the Municipal Fire Brigade a reward of some tens of millions, which has already been divided among the firemen, who were besides entertained to afternoon tea. A watch was kept all night and passed quietly. THANKS: "To all those who helped extinguish the fire at our refinery on the 11th of this month, and in particular to the Municipal Fire Brigade under its exceptionally brave, experienced and energetic chief Mr Voelpel, to the Voluntary Railway Brigade, to the military emergency detachment of the 11th field artillery regiment, to Captain Suski, to the State Police of Knihinin town and Stanisławów, and to all others who gave help selflessly and at the risk of their own lives, we offer here our heartfelt thanks. GRIFFEL'S REFINERY."

**25 February 1928 — Drzewo Polskie, year II, Warsaw, 25 February 1928, no. 4**  
PROSPECTS FOR EXPORT TO ENGLAND. In recent years, in connection with the Polish-German tariff war, the export of Polish timber was directed for the most part to England. Since the conclusion of the German-Polish timber agreement the situation has changed fundamentally. Because of the high prices obtainable on the German market, Polish export has oriented itself exclusively towards Germany, all the more so as prices on the English market have remained at the low level of earlier years. In this connection we turned to THE LARGEST GDAŃSK EXPORTER OF POLISH TIMBER TO ENGLAND, MR ZYGMUNT GRIFFEL, CO-OWNER OF "OVERSEAS TIMBER TRADING COMPANY LTD", with a request for his opinion on the prospects for Polish timber export to England. Mr Griffel kindly gave us the following information. For the moment trade with England is on a very modest scale. The causes are to be found in the high prices of Polish raw material (round timber), which bear no relation to raw-material prices in the Baltic countries — Latvia, Estonia, even Finland. Polish timber, which was always cheaper than that of the Baltic countries, is today far dearer if it has to be sold to England. This follows both from the end of the tariff war with Germany, which caused a rise on the Polish market, and from the enormous domestic demand, chiefly from the state railways, which this year bought more timber than in the previous six years together. Stocks in England are overflowing; the main cause is the English coal strike of 1926, which caused a hundred per cent rise in sea freight rates, so that supplies bought by England in Finland and Sweden were not brought in during 1926. Those stocks, and the quantities produced in Finland and Sweden in 1927, were shipped to England in 1927; and since, with the fall in building activity, demand in England itself was not large this year, English importers, especially on the east coast, hold large stocks and are in no hurry to buy the 1927/28 production. This year, given strong German demand for Polish timber and rising domestic demand, Polish producers are hardly cutting English dimensions at all, so that in a few months a shortage of certain dimensions may be felt in England — dimensions which only Poland can supply at any season, even in winter when all the northern ports are closed. Then some levelling of prices on the English market will follow, which must adjust to raw-material costs and to export prices in the timber-producing countries.

**March 1930 — Pracownik Drzewny: organ of the trade union of clerks of the timber industry of the Republic of Poland, year 6, no. 3 (March 1930)**  
THEY ARE CUTTING WORKERS' WAGES BY 10 PER CENT. The dance was started, as usual, by the firm "Glaesinger" (Dolina-Krechowice) with its notorious director Pohr at its head. The noble firm uses a tried system of corruption: cutting the wages of all the workers by 10 per cent, it introduces at the same time for a small handful the so-called "laufende Schichten", under which a worker is paid not only for effective working days but also for Sundays and holidays, that is for 30 days a month — so that he does not suffer from the cut but actually gains by it. A MERCHANT TURNS DETECTIVE. Lwów (from our correspondent). Bolechów, a small town in the Subcarpathian region, is a centre of the timber industry. Besides sawmills there are export firms sending timber to every country in the world. Even before the war the export firm "LEIZOR GRIFFEL" was known on international markets; SINCE THE FATHER'S DEATH IT IS RUN BY HIS SON SZAJA. The Griffel firm is in commercial contact with France and even has its own representative in Paris, a certain Zimmermann, who settles supply contracts on the spot and sends the money in the form of cheques on Paris to Griffel at Bolechów. In May Zimmermann carried out a transaction in Paris on Griffel's behalf for 14,000 francs. He sent two cheques by letter, one for 10,000 francs and the other for 4,000. And a strange thing happened: one of those letters, containing the cheque for 4,000 francs, went missing. Griffel telephoned Paris and established beyond doubt that the cheque had been sent. Full of anxiety he went to Lwów, at random, trying his luck. He went first to the "black exchange", reasoning that if someone had stolen the cheque at the post office he had either tried to cash it or had already cashed it there. The dark little Stanisław street, just off Lwów's main artery, is full of suspicious, distrustful figures willing to earn a few złoty on an exchange. Griffel, who often had larger amounts of currency to change himself, was known among these open-air "bankers". The rounds began: "Have you seen a cheque for 4,000 francs?" And — astonishingly — one was actually found to whom a stranger had offered exactly such a cheque the day before. "Yes," he told Griffel, "yesterday a stranger came to me with a cheque for 4,000 francs. I have not bought it yet. But he is to come again today. Wait, Mr Griffel. He will be here shortly." The scene was cleverly arranged: the money-changer was to be the middleman and the supposed buyer none other than Griffel himself. In this way the cheque returned to its lawful owner, and the police found one more post-office "mouse". The arrested man, Emil Jaworski, a post-office clerk of the ninth service grade, comes from Halicz. He is married, lives in Lwów at ul. Paulinów 12, formerly worked in the Directorate and latterly in the newspaper section at the main station. Where did he get the cheque? A tangled story. "I was," says Jaworski, "the day before yesterday in the Venetian café. I was playing dominoes. But a party got up for poker. I wanted to win something. I sat down at the table with 45 groszy, and when I got up I was 700 złoty in pocket. One of the players, the merchant Pępniakiewicz, had no cash. He handed me a cheque for 4,000 francs, signed it on the back and told me I could cash it in the morning wherever I liked. But neither at Gruess's nor at Schuetz and Chajes's would they take it. I came to the black market and here I was arrested. I do not know what for. I am innocent." The police, however, were not trusting. While one of the commissioners conducted the first interrogation, detectives searched the arrested man's home. The results were sensational. In the post-office clerk's house they found a whole store of opened foreign letters, a considerable collection of stamps torn from envelopes, a forged military book in which private first class Emil Jaworski had added a school-leaving certificate under "studies", and a second forged identity card of the Legionaries' Union in which the same private had promoted himself to major of the general staff in retirement, and so on. **31 December 1932 — Chwila, Saturday 31 December 1932, No. 4946**  
JUDGMENT OF THE SUPREME COURT IN THE CASE OF THE OIL FIRMS E. CH. GRIFFEL AND CO. CONCERNING 7,100 SHARES OF THE KORNEUBURGER MINERAL-ÖL-RAFFINERIE. Nowy Dziennik reports: in 1930, after the failure of the Griffel firms, the Vienna industrialists Izydor Adlersberg and Dr Zygfryd Adlersberg brought suit before the District Court in Kraków against the estate of the late EISIG CHAIM GRIFFEL for the surrender of 7,100 shares of one of the largest refineries in Austria, at Korneuburg. The Adlersbergs based their claim on the fact that the late Mr Griffel had deposited those shares in third hands as security for a loan of several tens of thousands of dollars received from them. The District Court in Kraków upheld the claim and ordered the estate to surrender the shares, and the Appeal Court in Kraków confirmed that judgment. On review, however, the Supreme Court altered both lower judgments and dismissed the plaintiffs, ordering them to pay the costs of the dispute. Holding the defendant's review to be justified, the Supreme Court expresses the following legal opinion: an agreement of the kind the late E. Ch. Griffel concluded with the plaintiffs — establishing on the disputed shares a pledge in the plaintiffs' favour of which they could make use only in the event of the debtor's bankruptcy — could not validly create a right of pledge, because that agreement excluded the essential purpose of a pledge under § 447 of the civil code, namely the right to obtain satisfaction from the pledged thing if the obligation is not performed in the appointed time, unless bankruptcy proceedings were opened. And in the event of bankruptcy the agreement secured the plaintiffs an exceedingly advantageous position to the detriment of other creditors. On the strength of this judgment the Adlersbergs' claim will not only not be privileged, but the shares saved from sale, representing half the total share capital of the Korneuburg refinery, will be used for the benefit of the creditors as a whole.

AROUND THE GRIFFEL AFFAIR IV. THE LOT OF THE FIRM'S EMPLOYEES. As we have written in previous articles, the present chairman of the liquidation committee and former composition administrator Dr Rychlewski collected during his term (from December 1932) over a million złoty, out of which he first of all covered his own remuneration and expenses. Under the act of 1933 the maximum monthly remuneration of a composition administrator is 3,000 zł, and as that sum is too small for Dr Rychlewski he found another way: while staying "on estate business" outside Kraków he receives allowances of 100 zł a day — and it need hardly be added that he therefore spends most of his time outside Kraków, always finding a reason for it. The administrator's claims are indeed privileged, but there are other claims which by law enjoy priority, namely the wages of the estate's employees. But the administration of the Griffel estate finds a way round the law in this as in other matters. AT THE REFINERY IN STANISŁAWÓW AND AT THE ESTATE'S MINES IN DŹWINIACZ SEVERAL EMPLOYEES ARE ENGAGED TO WHOM THE ADMINISTRATION PAYS NO MONTHLY WAGES AT ALL. THE EMPLOYEES IN STANISŁAWÓW ARE OWED 13 MONTHS' WAGES, AND THOSE IN DŹWINIACZ 23! Instead of money Dr Rychlewski sends them from time to time a consoling letter expressing the "hope of transferring more considerable advances in the coming days" and drawing attention to "the necessity of understanding the difficult situation in which the composition estate now finds itself" and similar phrases. Yes, counsellor! The estate's situation is indeed difficult, if three employees at Dźwiniacz are not paid wages amounting together to 260 zł a month, while one draws 3,000 zł oneself. It is curious that all the employees' requests and complaints to the labour inspector, to the district office at Nadwórna, and even to the president of the district court in Kraków remain without effect, and the administration neither pays nor dismisses. Nothing remains for the employees but to sue the estate for their wages. But to conduct a lawsuit with the Griffel estate is no easy thing, for it lasts years, especially as the papers go from the court in Kraków for the Griffels to be heard, and lie there for months undealt with. There have been cases where employees voluntarily reduced their claims by even half rather than sue the estate, but the administration prefers to pay legal costs which often exceed the value of the claim, solely in order to drag the matter out. By way of example we cite the case of two former employees who repeatedly and vainly asked for a settlement, declaring themselves ready to reduce their claims considerably, and were finally obliged to bring an action in the district court at Sołotwina. And now begins the curious course of a lawsuit in which the estate's counsel uses every possible means to prolong the dispute — and of course to increase his fee correspondingly. At the first hearing the Griffels' counsel objected that the Sołotwina court lacked jurisdiction, and the judge upheld the objection. The plaintiffs appealed to the district court in Stanisławów, which allowed the appeal and ordered the Griffel estate to pay the costs. The case therefore returned to the court at Sołotwina. Now the estate applied to be granted poor person's rights in the dispute, since it "possesses no property, for the liabilities considerably exceed the assets". When poor person's rights were refused, the Griffels' counsel in Kraków (Dr Arnold) appealed to the district court in Stanisławów, stating that "the defendant estate possesses no property, since the whole property has been handed over to the creditors' committee to cover the estate's debts" — this was in 1932, and as is known the property has to this day still not been handed over. The district court did not allow that appeal either, and finally the court at Sołotwina gave judgment (II C 245/31 of 18 May 1932) granting the plaintiffs their claims together with 10 per cent interest and the costs of the dispute. Against that judgment the Griffels again appealed to the district court in Stanisławów, objecting that the defendant — the curator of the estate DAWID MENDEL GRIFFEL — had not been heard. (The said person, despite repeated summonses, did not appear at the hearing in Kraków.) The district court rejected the appeal and the plaintiffs at last had a valid judgment. But even then the administration, despite the employees' pleas, would not pay them, and only when they obtained execution against the immovable property and the inventory of the mine — part of which was sold for a song at auction — were they paid what was due. As we see, this lawsuit ran three times in the district court at Sołotwina and three times in the district court at Stanisławów on appeal, and each time the estate was ordered to pay costs. Moreover in this small case three counsel were engaged on the side of the Griffel estate — one in Kraków (who directed the whole course of the matter), a second at Sołotwina and a third at Stanisławów; as many were engaged for the plaintiffs, and through losing the case the administration had to pay all six advocates. All this at the expense of the creditors, who instead of the return of their lent money receive a statement of the costs of administering the estate. The estate conducts several dozen such lawsuits with employees, and only in rare cases does it win. Are these lawsuits known to the judicial supervisory authorities in Kraków, and why are they tolerated?

Our readers will remember how many tears and misfortunes the Griffels' bankruptcy brought with it, how many thousands of people lost their fortunes or savings gathered by the sweat of their brow. Well-known banking institutions in Little Poland were also seriously involved in the Griffels' business. THE GRIFFEL OIL UNDERTAKING IN 1930 was an extraordinarily ramified centre of the oil industry, its establishments covering not only several places in Little Poland but abroad, namely Austria and Romania. It comprised: the Skawina Refinery at Skawina, the First Stanisławów Petroleum Factory at Stanisławów, the "Wierbiąż" refinery near Kołomyja, the "Babetta" oil shaft at Dźwiniacz, the "Dobra Nadzieja" mine at Dźwiniacz, and a series of oil terrains at Bitków, Pasieczna, Pniów, Starunia, Babcze, Maniawa, Majdan, Dźwiniacz, Rosulna; 50 per cent of the shares of the Korneuburger Mineralölraffinerie A.G. in Austria; and terrains in Romania. Besides this the Griffels had private property. THE WHOLE PROPERTY OF THE FIRM GRIFFEL REPRESENTED IN 1930 A VALUE OF 7,734,672 zł. Such a considerable undertaking employed in July 1930 more than 30 clerks. The centre of the undertaking was Stanisławów, where THE LATE CHAIM EISIG GRIFFEL AND HIS SON SZULIM GRIFFEL held office. THE AFFAIRS OF THE KORNEUBURG REFINERY WERE HANDLED BY THE SECOND SON, JAKÓB GRIFFEL; THE THIRD SON, MENDEL GRIFFEL, DEALT WITH TECHNICAL MATTERS; AND THE YOUNGEST, OZJASZ HENRYK, WORKED IN HIS FATHER'S OFFICE. The undertaking commanded considerable capital in the years 1917-1922 and in that period acquired a whole series of oil terrains, mines and refineries. After the collapse of the Polish mark in 1924 the firm was already, by 1926, under water — that is, working at a loss. Relying, however, on the profitable refineries at Stanisławów and Skawina, the Griffels not only did not confine themselves to balancing the books but took steps towards an enormous expansion of their undertaking, engaging — for want of sufficient capital of their own — other people's capital. The investments they undertook were enormous, considering the purchase of the "Dobra Nadzieja" mine and entry into the Romanian business and the Korneuburg refinery, where 60,000 dollars were paid out and a further 32,000 dollars were to be paid to start that refinery up. To obtain such great sums the Griffels drew into circulation outside capital, both from banks and from private persons. As cover and security for the money accepted, promissory notes or endorsed remittances were issued, while mortgage security on real property was scrupulously avoided — which made a favourable impression on lenders as to the firm's active financial state and made borrowing easier. These loans were taken at very high interest, 18 to 24 per cent a year, the interest as a rule being paid in advance. Both the high interest paid in advance and the clean mortgage register, and the large trade turnover visible from outside, gave the firm the appearance of a solid and solvent house and a convenient place to make capital fruitful — an appearance the Griffels zealously maintained. When confidence in their own signatures began to waver, they drew into circulation the remittances of other firms incapable of covering their obligations and, taking advantage of local ignorance of those firms' payment standing, put their remittances into circulation, strengthening them with their own endorsement. The organisation of the undertaking itself was conducted so that only those in whose interest it lay could understand the state of its affairs: NO COMMERCIAL BOOKS WERE KEPT AND NO BALANCE SHEETS WERE DRAWN UP AT ALL. In this state of affairs the Griffels filed on 17 July 1930 in the Kraków court an application for composition proceedings over their property, offering creditors 50 per cent of their claims. The passive state of the composition estate at that time amounted to 16,250,953 złoty. Comparing that figure with the active state gives a shortfall of over eight and a half million złoty; the firm could therefore in the summer of 1930 cover at most 50 per cent of its obligations. Then came the crash. The Griffels began to save themselves, being unable to meet their obligations. Unclean business was done right and left. They wanted at all costs to save themselves from ruin, taking no account of the fact that by their careless policy they had made thousands of poor people wretched, whose savings melted in the pockets of dishonest calculators. It came to the point where the prosecutor took an interest. A long and laborious investigation gave concrete results. The Griffels' trial has been fixed for 8, 9 and 10 July. In the dock will sit SCHULIM GRIFFEL, Leizor Weisblum, HENRYK GRIFFEL, Markus Blaustein and Leon Horowitz. Several dozen witnesses have been summoned to the trial, which arouses great interest not only in Stanisławów but throughout Little Poland, and is already the subject of general conversation in our town, where most of those harmed live.

The Griffel oil undertaking was in 1930 an extraordinarily ramified centre of the oil industry, reaching beyond Little Poland and abroad. It comprised the Skawina Refinery at Skawina and much else. The whole property of the firm represented in 1930 a value of 7,734,672 zł. Commanding considerable capital, in 1917-1922 the undertaking acquired a series of oil terrains, mines and refineries, in which it began to invest after 1926. These investments were enormous: the investment in the "Dobra Nadzieja" mine and the starting up of the Korneuburg refinery cost 92,000 dollars. To obtain such sums outside capital was engaged, from banks and from private persons. As cover and security, promissory notes or endorsed remittances were issued, while mortgage security was avoided, which made a favourable impression as to the firm's active financial state and eased borrowing. The loans were taken at very high interest, 18 to 24 per cent a year. Then, like a bolt from a clear sky, came the news of 17 July 1930 that the Griffels had applied for composition proceedings, offering creditors 50 per cent. The passive state was then 16,250,953 zł, giving a shortfall of over eight and a half million. The firm could cover at most 50 per cent of its obligations. Then came the crash. Unclean business was done right and left until the prosecutor took an interest. As a result of the investigation those held responsible are: the brothers SCHULIM AND OZJASZ GRIFFEL, resident in Kraków at Aleja Krasińskiego 20, Leizor Weisblum, Markus Blaustein and Leon Horowitz.

MOB JUSTICE AGAINST THE GRIFFELS. Stanisławów, 9 July. On the second day of the trial the court proceeded to examine one of the chief witnesses, MAJER HALLER, industrialist and former mayor of Sołotwina, who as a result of the Griffels' speculation became literally a beggar. The witness had traded with the Griffels since 1922 and had bought oil terrains jointly with them. He testifies bitterly that he suffered great losses and was materially ruined altogether. He not only put money into the oil terrains but supplied the Griffels with 5,300 dollars in cash, for which he received promissory notes. He had to put those notes into circulation and then redeem them himself, because the Griffels did not redeem them. "I became a poor man," says Haller before the tribunal, "and they" — pointing at the accused — "are well off and doing well." The Griffels' machinations were various. The witness testifies that, for example, he issued notes for at most 3,000 zł, while the Griffels, having notes signed in blank, raised the amount, paid the stamp duty at the tax office and put them into circulation. He thought his accommodation notes handed to the Griffels came to at most 5,000 dollars; meanwhile the Griffels raised the sum on their own initiative to about 30,000 dollars and put them into circulation. HOW THE GRIFFELS EARNED … Stanisławów. The second day of the trial was devoted to further witnesses. After Haller the court examined ADOLF HOROWITZ, retired director of the Stanisławów branch of the Mortgage Bank. In May, the director begins, he was approached as director of the Mortgage Bank to advance money against the Griffels' goods, supposedly cylinder oil. The Korneuburg refinery had ordered 200 tonnes of that oil from the Griffels and they had sent 14 tank-cars of it to that refinery. On behalf of his chiefs the accused Weissblum intervened with the witness, asking for the goods to be pledged. Having obtained information about the value of cylinder oil and examined the whole matter, he agreed, on a merchant's reasoning, to pay the Griffels 7,100 dollars, to be recovered after the Korneuburg refinery had redeemed the goods. He therefore ordered the sum paid, acting in good faith. A few days later he received word from Vienna that the Korneuburg refinery had not redeemed the goods. This gave him much to think about; he supposed something was wrong. He was not alarmed, however, since cylinder oil had at that time an established market price. Questioned about the non-redemption, Weissblum explained that it was merely the revenge of the firm Adlersberg of Vienna, with which the Griffels had for some time been in competitive conflict. The accused Szulim Griffel reassured the witness in the same spirit. Despite these assurances the witness wrote to director Adlersberg, a good acquaintance, for an explanation. Adlersberg replied asking for a sample of the oil, which was at that time standing at the Czech border at Bohumín. A clerk was sent from Stanisławów to draw oil from the tank-car and the sample was sent to Vienna, whence after a few days came word that the goods were of little value. The alarmed witness then sent a sample to the experimental station in Warsaw for expert examination. THE RESULT WAS UNEXPECTED: THE TANK-CARS CONTAINED NOT CYLINDER OIL BUT CARRIAGE OIL, THE SO-CALLED "NIEDOBEN", CHEAPER BY THREE AND A HALF DOLLARS THAN CYLINDER OIL. The Mortgage Bank could not therefore realise the oil; it was sold only a year later, and on that transaction with the Griffels about 6,000 dollars were lost. The witness had not sought business with the Griffels, knowing that their promissory-note debts ran to three million dollars. He had believed from the consignment notes that the oil was cylinder oil and had not supposed the Griffels would so deceive him. He added that he regarded the accused Szulim Griffel as the director of his father's business, since the late Eisig Chaim Griffel did not concern himself with business. For the 7,100 dollars paid out the Mortgage Bank received a guarantee from LEISOR GRIFFEL, but it was overlooked that the security was worthless, since instead of the three signatures required by the register only two were given. Next the bookkeeper of the Savings Bank, Maria Biczyńska, testified, after which a break was ordered. At the afternoon hearing REGINA FREILÓWNA, a private clerk, testified. She states that in May 1930 the intermediary Ehrlich came to her offering for discount promissory notes of Efraim Rothblum endorsed by Griffel. Through the same Ehrlich she had once already lent 500 dollars, and now, lending money on Rothblum's notes, she asked whether the firm was good; to which the intermediary declared: "it is first-rate". Meanwhile the Griffels' bankruptcy followed, and Freilówna turned to her advocate in Kraków, who established from the probate papers that the acceptor Rothblum, who was already dead, had left property showing 1,025 zł of assets and 1,058 zł of liabilities. After Rothblum's death — he had owed the Griffels 1,000 dollars — his son-in-law Horowitz began signing notes for his father-in-law. Dawid Feil, brother of the previous witness, the intermediary Pinkas Wagszal and Stefania Mannheim also testified. The hearing was then adjourned. Today about 20 more witnesses will be examined in the morning; in the afternoon the prosecutor and defenders will speak, and the verdict is expected late at night.

INCIDENTS AT THE GRIFFEL TRIAL. A SHORT CIRCUIT IN THE COURT BUILDING. Stanisławów, 11 July. We reported that the trial was set down for three days; it turned out, however, that the material could not be exhausted in that time, so the trial is being prolonged. According to this witness, in 1930 the accused Weissblum came and, producing three promissory notes for 20,500 Swiss francs, asked for credit for the Griffels. Acting in good faith and regarding the Griffels as an excellent firm, he sent the notes for rediscount to the Bank of Poland, placing on them the endorsement of the Municipal Savings Bank. THE GRIFFELS, HOWEVER, REMOVED THOSE CROSSINGS-OUT AND PUT THE NOTES INTO CIRCULATION IN KRAKÓW, WHERE THEY WERE DISCOUNTED BY THE BANK ZACHODNI. "I did not suppose," testifies the witness, "that so solid a firm, as I considered the Griffels to be, would take in our institution. We had full confidence in them. When the Griffels went bankrupt it came to my knowledge that some of the savings bank's notes were in circulation. I looked into it and established that this was in fact so. I therefore went at once to Kraków where the Griffels were staying and demanded explanations categorically. **16 July 1935 — Kurier Powszechny, year VIII, Kraków-Lwów, Tuesday 16 July 1935, No. 194**  
STANISŁAWÓW CHRONICLE. THE EPILOGUE OF A GREAT FRAUD. On 8 July the trial began before the criminal section of the local district court against members of the family of the late EISIG CHAIM GRIFFEL — the epilogue of the million-scale fraud and bankruptcy of the firm Griffel in 1930. Despite the passing of five years the case has not ceased to interest local society, especially Jewish society, not only because of the great number of those harmed but because of the five-year composition period, which led to nothing, since those harmed have to this day not received even part of their claims. The Griffels were owners of refineries at Stanisławów and Skawina and of oil terrains in the Nadwórna district, co-owners of the "Babet" mine at Dźwiniacz and the "Wierbiąż" refinery at Kołomyja. Despite such great property the firm had worked at a loss since 1926, which did not prevent the owners from expanding and even engaging foreign capital. It came to the point where the Griffels, commanding great foreign capital, sought to create a good impression of the activity of their balance sheet and so to inspire confidence among the local population. The plan succeeded completely. As the appearance of prosperity was magnified, so grew the trust of the wide Jewish masses, who began to place their savings with the firm. To what degree the Griffels were able to bemuse their co-religionists is shown by the fact that those placing savings sought no guardianship security in banks or mortgages: an ordinary scrap of paper from the firm sufficed. As trust grew, so did the firm's obligations, and it had to turn money obtained from others to satisfying and paying interest on debts already contracted. No wonder that not only the Jewish poor but the intelligentsia, and various banks and institutions, fell for the "safe and good" investment. It is characteristic that faith in Griffel's business was supported and upheld by the late miracle-working rabbi of Ottynia, Heger, who enjoyed great authority among wide masses of his co-religionists. That the Griffels knew the real state of their affairs is shown by their commercial books, kept in such a way that outsiders could hardly make out the state of their finances. And although the bomb had finally to burst, the Griffels went on accepting deposits until July 1930. In that month they filed for composition proceedings, not forgetting a few days earlier to remove from Stanisławów, quietly and mostly by night, all their valuables and precious objects from their household furnishings. The Griffels also moved from Stanisławów to Kraków, where they declared insolvency before the court there, seeking thus to disappear from the eyes of the harmed Jewish population and to remove them, by the great distance, from influence over the proceedings. The efforts of Stanisławów advocates to have the estate transferred to Stanisławów were of no avail. The size of the fraud is shown by the figures: in the composition proceedings the passive state was fixed at about 14 million złoty, of which 9 million fell on Stanisławów and district. In the course of the proceedings the Griffels' property was valued at 5 million złoty and the debts at 13 million, so the deficit proved greater still. In the dock sat SZULIM GRIFFEL, OZJASZ GRIFFEL, Weisblum (the Griffels' general bookkeeping fixer), Blaustein, and Horowitz, the last a specialist in manufacturing promissory notes. The tribunal: vice-president Dr Cysarz presiding, with judges Słowik and Malinowski; prosecution by the head of the prosecutor's office, Goertz; defence by advocates Landau and Gestler of Lwów, Zasławski and Darm. The civil claim on behalf of some of those harmed is brought by advocate Jonas. A series of witnesses passed before the tribunal whose evidence was simply crushing for the accused — whether it was an old woman worn out by life who had placed her savings with the Griffels in the hope of material advantage, or representatives of the banks and institutions harmed: always the subject was the Griffels' clever fraudulent machinations and those of their pack of company clerks. The public that gathered in the great hall of the district court filled it to the brim with Jewish people recruited from the poor who had placed their savings with the Griffels. After the evidence, the prosecutor and the defence made a series of applications, and the tribunal, granting most of them, adjourned the trial indefinitely. And so, five years after the millions-scale frauds came to light, the moment of justice has again been put off. EXPORT OF BOX SETS TO PALESTINE. The chief market for the products of our box-making industry has in recent years become PALESTINE, to which the export of box sets steadily grows. It is known, however, that Romania, the most considerable exporter of box sets to Palestine besides Poland, delivers goods more cheaply, enjoying the freight preference given by its favourable geographical position. If Poland has nevertheless managed to hold its position on the Palestinian market and even strengthen it, this is owed on the one hand to the strenuous efforts of our producers, and on the other to the circumstance that Poland, as a considerable buyer of citrus fruit, has naturally become Palestine's "natural" counterpart in the export of sets for packing that fruit. Our export grows steadily, and the transactions concluded in 1937 are estimated at over 3 million box sets. That quantity is to be supplied by the Little Poland firms: Bracia Groedel of Skole, Małopolski Przemysł Drzewny of Synowódzko Wyżne, J. Ph. Glesinger of Broszniów, Izydor Landau of Czarny Dunajec, ZYGMUNT GRIFFEL OF LWÓW (Segal, Zupnik and Wilf), Engel and Huttrer of Lesko-Łukawica and Lichtmann of Dobromil, and two Warsaw firms, "Las Kresowy" and the Union of Cooperatives "Społem". On 18 December the season of orange export from Palestine began and the first shipments are already arriving. Importers are already remitting payment for the fruit. It should be noted that in Palestinian box sets there is no internal competition: the requirements are still such that only some of the better-equipped box works can meet them, so only 9 firms take part in the export to Palestine, which makes it possible to "hold" the price.
*timber export · orange crates to Palestine*
